## Map the Cassette PROM by Its Address Pins (#35)

### Purpose and scope

`#35` reported that `CASSETTE_ROM` in `main.js` does not disassemble as code at
`$FB00`, with both documented entry points, `$FBBC` and `$FC54`, in the middle
of instructions, and concluded that the array was a damaged or partly invented
transcription of `prom.ic10`. The symptom was real. The diagnosis was not.

The array is byte-identical to `amico2000_replica_PROM_IC10.bin` as
ComputerHistory.it serves it today (512 bytes, SHA-256 `0f9d27ff...6722`).
What was wrong is where the loader put it. A PROM image is in chip order: byte
N is the cell the chip selects when its own address pins carry N. A 512-cell
PROM has nine of those pins, A0-A8, so the cell that answers at an address is
the address's low nine bits. IC9 starts at `$FE00`, where they are all low,
and its image reads out in order. IC10 starts at `$FB00`, an odd page: A8 is
high across `$FB00-$FBFF` and low across `$FC00-$FCFF`, so the first 256 bytes
of the image belong at `$FC00` and the last 256 at `$FB00`.
`Amico2000._installROM()` copied both images in file order from the start of
the socket, which is right for IC9 and exchanges the two halves of IC10. The
issue's scan of load bases `$FAF0-$FB10` could not find that, because no
single base puts a page-swapped image right.

`_installROM()` now walks the socket and takes each address's cell from its
low nine bits. The same code serves both sockets, so the monitor is installed
exactly as before. This reaches `amico.loadCassetteROM()` and, through it, a
`prom.ic10` file chosen with **Load ROM or tape**, which had the same fault.

Deliberately unchanged:

- The `CASSETTE_ROM` array. Its comment now says that it is in chip order and
  must stay that way. The comment's stale "Load ROM" button name and its claim
  that cassette functionality needs the ROM are corrected in the same change,
  as `NextSession.md` asked, and the commented-out loader call is gone.
- The cassette traps and the `.amtape` path. They act before the CPU fetches
  from the socket, fitted or not.
- The default. The PROM is still not fitted at startup; whether it should be
  is recorded in `NextSession.md` as an owner decision.
- An image shorter than the chip is still accepted and still leaves the rest
  of the socket alone. Its bytes are now cells 0 upward, so for IC10 they
  appear from `$FC00` rather than `$FB00`. Nothing in the repository loads a
  short cassette image.

`AGENTS.md` gains a PROM Image Order section, and its Cassette Interface
Reference now records what the PROM adds to the manual's description of the
tape record and which port bits the routines use. `README.md` and the guide
say that a `prom.ic10` file is a dump in chip order. The `#31` test that
compared `$FB00` with `image[0]` passed before and after only because its test
pattern repeats every 256 bytes; it now indexes by cell.

The issue's three suggested actions were therefore not taken as written.
Replacing the array with the published binary would change no byte, dropping
it would discard a verified copy of a file the Wayback Machine does not hold,
and the changelog's "authentic" needs no correction.

### Verification

`tests/cassette-rom-mapping.test.js` adds seven checks. Five fail on the code
from before the fix; the hash check and the trap check pass either way, as
they should, since neither depends on the mapping. `node --test tests/` is 97
checks, all passing. `node --check` passes on `amico2000.js`, `main.js` and
the changed test files, and `git diff --check` is clean.

The mapping is held to evidence the loader cannot influence:

- The manual. The supplement prints what REG shows at each entry point:
  `FBBC D8` on page 47 and `FC54 A9` on page 46 (the extracted text has the
  second as `AY`; the page scan was read to settle it). A test stores each
  address at `$00F6/$00F7`, presses REG on the running monitor and decodes the
  display through the ROM's own segment table. In file order the bytes at
  those addresses were `0A` and `FB`.
- The code. Followed from the two entry points, the mapped PROM is 238
  instructions covering 502 of its 512 bytes, with no undefined opcode and no
  transfer into the middle of an instruction. The other ten bytes are `$FF`
  filler at `$FCF6-$FCFF`. This is the verification the issue asked for, kept
  as a test. Its instruction-length table is checked against the CPU core's
  own decode table, and a control run on the file-order image fails at once.
- Execution. With the trap off, the real SAVE routine runs to completion from
  `$FBBC`, across `$FBFF` into page `$FC`, and out through `JMP $FE22`. For
  the manual's own example, `$0010-$001F` as program 13, it writes 256 zero
  bytes, `$A5`, `13 10 00 0F 00`, the sixteen data bytes, a checksum of the
  lot, and 256 zero bytes: the record drawn in Fig. 27. Port A shows `$05`,
  `$06`, `$05` in turn, which is the LED sequence the manual describes if
  bits 0-2 are D4-D6. The leader lasts 6.17 emulated seconds, where the manual
  says the LEDs change "dopo sei secondi circa", and the rate is 332 bit/s,
  where it says about 300. A 1KB save takes 37.2 seconds; the manual says
  about 45, which was not investigated.
- The hardware. The published dump of decoder PROM IC7 enables the IC10
  socket on pages `$FB` and `$FC` and no others, and IC6 enables IC9 on `$FE`
  and `$FF`. The published IC9 dump is also byte-identical to `MONITOR_ROM`.

Reading the real routines showed that the traps differ from them in what they
leave in `$0000-$0004`. That is outside this issue and is listed in
`NextSession.md`, along with the dead Wayback links to the PROM dumps in
`docs/computerhistory-amico2000.md`.

Browser check, in headless Chrome on a scratch copy of `index.html` with the
CSP meta tag removed so that an inline check script could run: the published
`prom.ic10`, given to the page's own file input, was accepted by the loader
("Loaded: prom.ic10 (512 bytes)"); the keypad then showed `FBBC D8` after AD
FBBC and `FC54 A9` after AD FC54, where the empty socket had shown `FBBC FF`;
the manual's sequence (AD 00F6 DA BC + FB, then REG) showed `FBBC D8`; and RUN
from there still went to the file-backed trap and stored a tape record. Keys
were dispatched as keyboard events and the CPU was stepped by the script,
because `requestAnimationFrame` does not fire under virtual time. Not tested:
a person using the real file picker, and Safari or Firefox.

## Redraw the Key Caps From Photographs of Real Boards

### Purpose and scope

The owner asked for the on-screen board to be brought much closer to the real
machine, and supplied two photographs of surviving boards to work from. They
are called unit A (powered, displays lit, taken at an angle) and unit B
(unpowered, sharp, taken from straight above) here and in the code comments.
The photographs are private: they are not in the repository, and only
measurements and colour samples taken from them are. A plan of seven visual
changes was agreed, each to be its own commit. This is the first.

The page drew blank caps with the legend silkscreened beneath each one, and a
comment said the real board does the same. Both photographs show otherwise:
the legends are printed in white on the caps. The earlier reading came from
600-pixel photographs.

What changed, all in `index.html`:

- Each key is a square cap drawn in SVG: four bevels, a slightly dished face,
  lit from the upper left as unit B is. The artwork is one `<symbol>` reused
  by all 23 keys; a key's colours come from custom properties on its button.
- The colours are sampled from unit B: red `#c61827` and blue `#0c80b1` on the
  faces, with separate values for each bevel. The blue had been a royal blue,
  `#2d5eae`.
- The legends are on the caps. Their size and width were measured on unit B
  for every key: the lettering is about a third of the cap tall, the function
  names and A-F close to the condensed DIN cut and the digits close to the
  regular one. The page's CSP forbids a downloaded font, so the legends use
  stacks of system faces, and each legend's `textLength` pins its width so a
  substitute face still fills the same space. The increment key's arrow is
  drawn as a path.
- The hex block and the function block now share one switch pitch, 4.36% of
  the board's width (about 13 mm), and their bottom rows are in line. Before,
  the function keys were wider than the hex keys. Both blocks are placed from
  the bottom edge, so they stay in line when the board's proportions change in
  a later step.
- The printed square under each switch is a hairline around the cap. On unit B
  the squares are separate and barely larger than the caps.
- A pressed key darkens and loses most of its shadow. `main.js` marks a pressed
  key by setting an inline `translateY` on its button, for the mouse, for touch
  and for the computer keyboard alike; the pressed style is keyed to that
  inline style, so all three get the same feedback and the keyboard handling
  is not touched. The old rule named a class, `is-down`, that nothing ever set.

Deliberately unchanged: `Amico2000.keyMap`, the 23 `data-key` values and their
order, the `aria-label` and `title` of every key, `main.js`, and the machine
layer. One invented disc capacitor that the function keys now cover was
removed. The NOR/SS switch keeps its old look until its own step.

`AGENTS.md` said the legends are silkscreened on the board; it now says they
are printed on the caps.

### Verification

`node --test tests/` passes, including `tests/keyboard-matrix.test.js`, and
`git diff --check` is clean. No JavaScript file changed.

Browser check, in headless Chrome 2x against `python3 -m http.server 8000`,
driven over the DevTools protocol with real mouse and key events:

- All 23 keys are present with their original `data-key` values, and each is
  the topmost element at its own centre, so nothing overlaps a key.
- With the mouse: RES returns to the monitor; AD 0 2 A 5 shows `02A5`; DA 3 C
  stores `$3C` there; the increment key shows `02A6`; the remaining hex keys
  typed as addresses read back correctly, so all sixteen were exercised; RUN
  starts a small program at `$0200` and RES recovers it; REG and HLT do not
  throw.
- With the keyboard: ArrowUp 0 3 F E ArrowDown 7 7 shows `03FE 77`, and a held
  key moves its on-screen cap and releases it.
- A held key takes the pressed style and gets its shadow back on release.
- No console error, exception or CSP report.

The legends were compared with unit B by measuring the ink box of every legend
on both, as a percentage of its cap: heights agree within one point and widths
within about two. The key blocks were compared side by side with the same

Not tested: touch input, Safari and Firefox, and any system without the two

## Pin the Display's Segment Mapping Before Redrawing It

### Purpose and scope

The second agreed change redraws the six displays, and `display.js` is the
only JavaScript in the plan that runs every frame. It had no check of any
kind: it draws into the DOM, which Node does not have. Before touching it,
`tests/display-renderer.test.js` pins what a redrawn display must not get
wrong.

The test gives `display.js` a stand-in for the few DOM calls it makes
(`getElementById`, `createElementNS`, attributes, `classList`,
`appendChild`), builds a real `DisplayRenderer` on it, and checks:

- one SVG, six digits, eight segments each, and each segment answering to its
  pattern bit: a to g on bits 0 to 6 and the decimal point on bit 7;
- each bit lights its own segment and no other, and each of the six patterns
  goes to its own digit;
- the digits are drawn left to right, on an even pitch, with a gap between the
  four address digits and the two data digits;
- the monitor's digit table, read from the ROM image at `$FFEA`, draws the
  sixteen hex digits a seven-segment reference gives;
- `update()` with fewer than six patterns, `clear()`, `test()` and
  `showHex()` behave as their names say;
- the display a booted monitor produces reaches the renderer as `0000 00`;
- nothing the renderer creates refers to an `http:` or `data:` resource, which
  the page's CSP would block.

One function in the test, `isLit()`, knows how the renderer marks a lit
segment, and one, `digitX()`, knows how it places a digit. Everything else
speaks in segment names, so the redraw should need to change only those two.

No code outside `tests/` changed. `AGENTS.md` and `NextSession.md` listed the
segment patterns as uncovered; they now say that the renderer's side is
covered and the machine layer's multiplexing is not.

### Verification

`node --test tests/display-renderer.test.js` passes all twelve checks against
the renderer as it stood. `node --test tests/` is 109 checks, all passing, and
`node --check` and `git diff --check` are clean.

The test was then run against three deliberately broken copies of `display.js`
in a scratch directory, to see that it can fail:

- the pattern bits of segments b and c exchanged: four checks fail;
- the digits filled right to left: the first draft of the test passed, because
  it only ever looked at `renderer.digits` in order. The left-to-right check
  was added for this and now fails on that copy;
- the decimal point never lit: two checks fail.

## Draw the Displays as Six Separate Packages

### Purpose and scope

Second of the seven agreed visual changes. The page drew the display as one
black bar holding six upright digits, with two always-lit dots between the
address and the data and the captions ADDRESS and DATA underneath. Unit B
shows six separate packages, which the manual's parts list names as TIL312
(IC18/1-6): four together, a gap about half a package wide where the board
shows through, then two. There is no colon and no caption on the board.

`display.js` is rewritten around measurements taken on unit B:

- A package face is 105 x 188 px on a 3000 x 1000 rectification of the
  photograph, on a 111 px pitch, with the fifth package 1.46 pitches after
  the fourth. In board terms the row runs from 75.0% to 98.8% of the width
  and from 16.8% to 35.6% of the height, hard against the right-hand edge.
- The digit is 43% of the package height and leans about 8 degrees. The
  photograph gives between 7 and 10 depending on which edge is measured.
- An unlit segment is dusty red with a paler rim against a near-black face
  (`#833854` on `#231d28` as sampled), so an idle digit reads as a faint 8
  drawn in outline. The emulator is always powered, so the unlit segments are
  drawn a little darker than the daylight photograph shows them, to keep lit
  digits unmistakable.
- Each package has two marks at the foot of the digit. The one at the lower
  left has the segments' tint and is the decimal point: the TIL312 is a
  left-decimal part, and the manual has the displays fitted with the point
  toward the keys. The one at the lower right is greyer, a mark in the
  moulding, and is drawn but is not a light. Nothing lights the decimal point
  in practice, because `Amico2000._updateDisplay()` drops bit 7; `test()`
  still can.
- Both photographs show a pale line between neighbouring packages. It is drawn
  as a narrow strip on the left of each face.

How it is built: one group of static artwork per package, and one shared layer
holding a lit copy of every segment, hidden until its bit is set. The glow
filter is applied once to that layer, with a fixed region, instead of once per
lit segment as before. `update()` still takes six patterns and is still called
once per frame by the machine layer, but it now compares each pattern with the
one it last drew and sets an attribute only on segments whose bit changed.

The lit colour and the glow are deliberately the old ones, rescaled to the new
units. They are the next change.

`index.html` loses the black bar, its gold pins and its padding, and places
the block at the measured position. The old maker legend at the right-hand
edge ran into the sixth package there, so it is set slightly smaller until it
is redrawn in its own step.

The geometry lives in one object, `DISPLAY_LAYOUT`, because the browser
scripts share a global scope and loose constants named `PITCH` or `DP` would
be easy to collide with.

### Verification

`tests/display-renderer.test.js` passes with one helper changed, `isLit()`,
which now reads the `visibility` attribute where it read the fill colour. The
table of which segments each pattern lights is untouched, and `digitX()` did
not need to change. One check is new: sixty identical frames write nothing to
the DOM, and changing one digit from 5 to 6 is a single write. `node --test
tests/` is 110 checks, all passing; `node --check display.js` and
`git diff --check` are clean.

In headless Chrome 2x the browser check from the previous entry passes
unchanged, which covers what the display shows after typing with the mouse and
the keyboard, read back from the machine. The drawing was compared with unit
B side by side, same window and same scale: the six packages land on the
photograph's within a few pixels, and the digit's height and the positions of
the two marks agree. A mixed pattern was also drawn at 6x: two digits with all
eight bits set, one with seven, one blank, then `1` and `C`.

Not tested: Safari and Firefox. The unlit segments' brightness is a judgement
between the daylight photograph of unit B, where they are plain, and the
powered photograph of unit A, where they are barely visible.

## Give Lit Segments the Colour and Bloom of the Real Displays

### Purpose and scope

Third of the seven agreed visual changes. Lit segments were `#ff2200`, an
orange-red, with a 2-unit blur behind each one. Unit A, photographed powered
and showing `0000 1C`, is the reference for what lit looks like:

- The colour is a deep red with no orange in it. The camera records it as
  `#fe085b` with a few near-white pixels at the hottest points. Sensors render
  the 660 nm of an early red LED pinker than the eye sees it, so the page uses
  the same hue pulled back a little, `#ff1f45`, as agreed with the owner in
  the plan.
- A lit segment is much fatter than its unlit window and has no sharp ends:
  the bars swell, the hairlines between neighbouring segments close, and the
  corners of a digit are round.
- A dim red halo lies on the package face around the digit.

In `display.js` a lit segment is now drawn with a round-jointed stroke of its
own colour, 3.2 units on a 10-unit bar, which widens it, rounds its points and
closes the gaps. The filter on the lit layer is two blurs instead of one: a
tight one (2.6 units) for the swelling and a wide one (9 units) at 60% for the
halo. It is still a single filter over the single lit layer, with a fixed
region, so nothing is recomputed while the display is steady.

A first attempt added a paler bar down the middle of each lit segment for a
hot centre. Side by side with unit A it read as a drawn line, which the
photograph does not show, so it was removed.

The indicator LEDs in `index.html` take the same red when lit, with the
near-white centre and halo that the lit LED on unit A shows. Their unlit look
and their arrangement are the next change.

### Verification

`git diff --check` are clean. The renderer test needed no change.

The lit display was put beside the same region of unit A at the same scale,
